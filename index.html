AI on Video Advertising: What It Can Do, and What It Still Can't

Artificial Intelligence

What AI Does Well in Video Advertising and Creative Production

AI is most persuasive when it is used to accelerate the work around the story, not pretend to be the story itself.

In video advertising, decisions are made quickly and replaced just as quickly. A colour choice, a hook, the length of a shot, the timing of an edit, the background music under a line, each of these variables is tested, revised, and reworked in a system built around speed, performance, and short attention spans. This is the environment in which Artificial Intelligence has found its strongest footing, because it thrives when the challenge is not emotional invention but production efficiency.

What AI Does Well in Video Advertising

The PDF makes that point clearly. AI has improved video advertising most convincingly in the areas of speed, scale, and repeatable execution. Platforms now demand constant content refreshes, multiple aspect ratios, and campaign variations across channels, and AI-powered tools help teams keep up with that volume. Instead of asking editors and producers to manually rebuild the same idea again and again, AI reduces the drag around routine work.

Automated Production & Toolsets

One of the clearest examples is automated production workflows. Tasks that once consumed long editing cycles, such as trimming footage, adjusting aspect ratios, generating captions, cleaning sound, or creating several versions of the same video, can now be completed rapidly through AI-assisted software. Tools like Adobe Sensei do not replace creative judgment, but they do remove a large amount of repetitive labour, which gives teams more time to refine narrative choices instead of managing mechanical edits.

AI also proves its value when a campaign has to live across multiple platforms at once. A social-first idea rarely stays in one form. It may need a vertical cut for mobile, an in-stream version for pre-roll, a shorter hook-led edit for paid social, and additional variations for testing. AI can resize, reframe, and re-edit the same base asset into multiple platform-specific outputs far faster than a traditional manual workflow. In performance-driven campaigns, that speed becomes a real operational advantage.

Low-Stakes and Functional Content

Another area where the technology works well is low-stakes or functional video content. Explainer videos, product demonstrations, onboarding materials, and internal brand communications do not always need emotional richness. They need clarity, consistency, and speed. AI-driven platforms can generate presentable videos for these purposes without the cost and logistics of a full production shoot, which makes them especially useful when the communication goal is straightforward and practical.

The same logic extends to short-form social content. The PDF notes that platforms like Pictory can convert blogs, scripts, or product descriptions into quick video assets for social feeds. These may not push creative boundaries, but that is not always the job. Their function is to keep the brand active, visible, and testable in crowded digital spaces where consistency and content velocity matter.

Accessibility & Localization

The article also highlights another strength that should not be overlooked: accessibility. Automated captioning, translations, voiceovers, and audio descriptions help brands extend reach across languages and abilities without carrying the same time and cost burden as older localisation workflows. In that sense, AI is not only making work faster, it is also making it easier for brands to create video advertising that is more inclusive, scalable, and practically usable.

Production Systems

Automated Workflows for Faster Video Advertising Teams

The real workflow gain is not just more output. It is more room for teams to think, test, and refine without drowning in repetition.

What makes AI genuinely useful to advertising teams is not simply that it can generate something quickly. Its deeper value is in how it reorganises production itself. The PDF frames this well by showing that the real shift is not one spectacular AI moment, but a chain of smaller efficiencies that stack together across post-production, distribution, and performance testing.

Managing Multi-Format Output at Scale

Modern ad teams are no longer producing one polished film for one destination. They are building systems of outputs: multiple lengths, multiple aspect ratios, multiple hooks, multiple calls to action, often for several platforms at the same time. In that context, one of AI's strongest contributions is that it turns a single base edit into a more flexible production engine. A single creative can become a family of assets without requiring editors to manually recreate each variation from scratch.

That is why automated production workflows matter so much. Tasks like trimming footage, producing cut-downs, generating captions, cleaning audio, or reframing material for vertical, square, and widescreen placements once took significant manual attention. AI-assisted software compresses much of that time. Instead of losing days to repetitive versioning work, teams can move from edit to deployment faster while maintaining consistency across outputs.

Speed, Scalability, and Creative Optimisation

The PDF also points to scalability as a key advantage. Social ecosystems demand constant adaptation. A campaign running across Instagram, YouTube, and TikTok needs to honour different viewing habits and formats while still preserving the same strategic core. AI helps by making that adaptation faster and more systematic. It supports mobile-first placements, short-form variations, and rapid iteration across channels without slowing the campaign rhythm.

This workflow advantage becomes even clearer in creative optimisation. Video advertising today is deeply tied to testing. Teams often need to compare multiple openings, multiple captions, multiple thumbnails, and several calls to action just to understand what performs best with different audience groups. AI can generate those combinations, analyse response signals in real time, and shorten the path between launch and learning. The result is a production process that becomes more responsive to data without becoming completely ruled by it.

Shifting Focus Back to Strategy and Quality

Functional content is another useful lens here. Product demos, explainers, internal communications, and onboarding videos are often less about emotional storytelling and more about speed, accuracy, and consistency. In those cases, AI-driven tools can create serviceable outputs with much lower overhead. That frees up production resources for the work that truly requires human interpretation and craft.

Seen this way, AI does not just make teams faster in a superficial sense. It changes where time is spent. Less effort goes into repetition, formatting, and rebuilds. More effort can go into approvals, strategy, creative refinement, and narrative quality. That is why automated workflows matter: they do not merely increase volume, they improve the working conditions under which stronger advertising decisions can be made.

Creative Limits

AI on Video Advertising: What It Can Do, and What It Still Can't

AI can optimise the surface of a video. Human judgment still decides whether that surface means anything.

The larger argument of the PDF is not that AI is useless in video advertising. It is that its usefulness has boundaries, and those boundaries become obvious the moment advertising moves from execution into meaning. AI performs exceptionally well when the objective is speed, optimisation, testing, or scale. It becomes much less reliable when the work requires emotional coherence, cultural sensitivity, and original narrative intent.

What AI Does Well in Video Advertising

That is because good video advertising is not made of polished images alone. It depends on tone, context, memory, and timing. Warmth, humour, nostalgia, tension, and cultural resonance are not simply visual effects that can be arranged like production assets. They are felt experiences. AI can often reproduce the surface signals associated with those feelings, but it cannot fully understand why a particular image carries emotional weight for an audience or why a certain line lands differently depending on cultural mood.

The PDF uses the example of a recent festive Coca-Cola campaign to illustrate this gap. The ad drew on AI-generated visuals to recreate familiar holiday imagery tied to the brand's long-established seasonal storytelling. The issue was not that the images were technically broken. The problem was that they felt emotionally incomplete. The campaign reproduced the look of sentiment, but not the emotional continuity viewers expected from the brand. That difference is where human creative judgment still matters most.

Where AI Falls Short

There is also the problem of cultural context. Video advertising never exists outside time and place. Strong campaigns often work because they understand a social moment, a regional nuance, or an audience's shared references. AI does not have lived experience. When it tries to speak into festivals, social movements, or specific aesthetics without human guidance, the result can become generic, overly safe, or misaligned with audience sentiment. Even when it avoids controversy, it may still fail quietly by missing the emotional register the audience expects.

At a structural level, AI's creative limitation comes from the way it learns. Generative systems are trained on existing data and existing patterns. In video advertising, that often means they become very good at recombining what has already worked. But effective brand-building requires distinctiveness, not just familiarity. When many brands use similar tools trained on overlapping datasets, their outputs risk converging into the same visual culture: similar transitions, similar pacing, similar storytelling cues, similar polish.

The Collaborative Hybrid Future

This is why the conclusion of the PDF matters. The future is not full automation, and it is not a rejection of AI either. It is a hybrid model. AI should support human creativity by handling execution, optimisation, and production efficiency, while people remain responsible for ideation, storytelling, and brand voice. That is the balance Enso 8 is aiming for: using AI as a collaborative tool that sharpens precision without flattening the emotional or cultural intelligence that strong advertising depends on.

In the end, the real question is not whether AI belongs in video advertising. It already does. The more important question is how it is used, and whether it is being used to create genuine value rather than simply perform innovation. The strongest future for video advertising is not AI versus creativity. It is AI and creativity working together, each doing what it does best.
